# Privacy, Anti-Spam and Data Protection for Canadian Small Businesses

This file covers the privacy and anti-spam rules a Toronto or Ontario small business meets when it collects customer data, sends marketing email or texts, or stores data in the cloud: PIPEDA, mandatory breach reporting, CASL, Ontario's health privacy law (PHIPA), Quebec's Law 25 and cross-border data storage. Tax and record-keeping rules are in [federal-requirements.md](federal-requirements.md); Ontario employment rules (including the electronic monitoring policy) are in [ontario-requirements.md](ontario-requirements.md).

**This is general information, not legal advice.** Pages were read on 2026-11-12; each page's "updated" date is given where it was shown.

## Who PIPEDA applies to in Ontario

From the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (OPC), [PIPEDA requirements in brief](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/pipeda_brief/) (updated 2024-05-01):

- PIPEDA applies to private-sector organizations that collect, use or disclose personal information **in the course of commercial activities**. Ontario has no general private-sector privacy law of its own, so a Toronto retailer, contractor, agency or online store is covered by PIPEDA for customer data.
- It also covers federally regulated businesses (banks, airlines, telecoms, broadcasters), including their **employees'** information, and any personal information that crosses provincial or national borders.
- Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec have private-sector laws deemed "substantially similar"; Ontario's substantially similar law covers **personal health information** only (PHIPA, below).
- Not-for-profits and charities are generally not covered unless they carry out commercial activities.

**Note:** For a provincially regulated Ontario business, PIPEDA generally does not cover its own employees' personal information (the OPC page lists employee information only for federally regulated organizations).

## PIPEDA's 10 fair information principles in practice

PIPEDA's Schedule 1 sets out 10 principles ([PIPEDA requirements in brief](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/pipeda_brief/)): accountability, identifying purposes, consent, limiting collection, limiting use/disclosure/retention, accuracy, safeguards, openness, individual access, and challenging compliance.

**Recommendation** (practical checklist for a small business, our interpretation of the principles):

| Principle | What it looks like in a small business |
|---|---|
| Accountability | Name one person responsible for privacy and put their contact on the website |
| Identifying purposes / consent | Say why you collect each field on forms (quote, booking, newsletter) and get consent for marketing separately |
| Limiting collection | Don't ask for birth dates, SIN or ID scans unless you need them |
| Retention | Set a deletion schedule for old leads and files, consistent with CRA record rules in [federal-requirements.md](federal-requirements.md) |
| Safeguards | Two-factor login on email, CRM and accounting; limit who can export customer lists |
| Openness | Publish a plain-language privacy policy |
| Access | Have a process to answer "what do you hold about me?" requests |

## Mandatory breach reporting under PIPEDA

From the OPC's [What you need to know about mandatory reporting of breaches of security safeguards](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/business-privacy/breaches-and-safeguards/privacy-breaches-at-your-business/gd_pb_201810/) (dated 2025-08-11):

- **Report to the OPC** any breach where it is reasonable to believe it creates a **real risk of significant harm (RROSH)**, whether one person or many is affected. Significant harm includes humiliation, reputational damage, financial loss, identity theft and damage to property. RROSH is assessed on the **sensitivity** of the information and the **probability** of misuse.
- **Notify affected individuals** as soon as feasible, directly (mail, email, phone) unless that would cause further harm or undue hardship, in which case a public notice may be used.
- **Notify other organizations** that can reduce harm, such as police or a payment processor.
- **Keep a record of every breach**, reported or not, for **two years** (longer if another law requires). Unreported breaches should record why there was no RROSH.
- Knowingly failing to report, notify or keep records is an offence that can lead to fines; the OPC refers cases to the Attorney General of Canada rather than fining directly.

**Recommendation:** Keep a one-page breach log (date, what happened, data involved, RROSH decision, who was notified). A lost laptop, a misdirected email with client files or a compromised email account all belong in it.

## CASL: consent rules for commercial emails and texts

Canada's anti-spam law (CASL) applies to commercial electronic messages (email, text, social direct messages) sent to or from Canada. Penalty maximums are summarized in [federal-requirements.md](federal-requirements.md). From ISED's [Getting consent to send email](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canada-anti-spam-legislation/en/getting-consent-send-email) (modified 2019-04-01):

| Consent type | How you get it | How long it lasts |
|---|---|---|
| Express | A clear yes, verbal or written, with a chance to decline | Until withdrawn |
| Implied: purchase or contract | Customer bought from you | Up to 2 years |
| Implied: inquiry or application | Person asked about your services | Up to 6 months |
| Implied: conspicuously published address | Address posted publicly without a "no solicitations" note | Only for messages related to the person's role or business |

- Senders should be ready to **prove consent**, so keep records (address, date, how consent was obtained).
- Charities and non-profits can rely on non-business relationships such as a donation, membership or volunteering.

**Recommendation:** Use express consent (an unticked checkbox with clear wording) for newsletters, because it does not expire. Tag implied-consent contacts with their purchase or inquiry date in your email tool so they can be dropped or re-permissioned before 2 years or 6 months.

## CASL: identification and unsubscribe requirements

Every commercial message must include ([Getting consent to send email](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canada-anti-spam-legislation/en/getting-consent-send-email)):

- your business name and the name of anyone you send on behalf of;
- a current **mailing address** plus a phone number, email address or website, valid for at least **60 days** after sending;
- an **unsubscribe mechanism** that is easy to use and free, with requests acted on within **10 business days**.

Mainstream email platforms (see [../08-software-directory/crm-and-marketing.md](../08-software-directory/crm-and-marketing.md)) add the footer and unsubscribe link automatically, but the business is still responsible for the consent of every contact it imports.

## Ontario health privacy: PHIPA

Ontario's Personal Health Information Protection Act (PHIPA) is recognized as substantially similar to PIPEDA for **personal health information** ([PIPEDA requirements in brief](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/pipeda_brief/)). It is overseen by the Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario (IPC).

**Unverified** (the IPC website blocked automated access on 2026-11-12, so these points were not checked against the official page): PHIPA applies to "health information custodians" such as physiotherapists, chiropractors, dentists, psychologists, massage therapists and other regulated health practitioners and clinics, and to their agents and IT providers. Custodians must notify individuals of privacy breaches, report certain breaches to the IPC, and file annual breach statistics with the IPC. Clinic owners should confirm these duties directly with the IPC (ipc.on.ca) before relying on them.

**Recommendation:** Clinics using practice-management software (for example Jane App, listed in [../08-software-directory/operations-and-productivity.md](../08-software-directory/operations-and-productivity.md)) should check the vendor's Canadian data hosting and breach-notification terms.

## Quebec's Law 25 for Ontario businesses with Quebec customers

From Quebec's privacy regulator (Commission d'accès à l'information), [Principaux changements – Loi 25](https://www.cai.gouv.qc.ca/protection-renseignements-personnels/sujets-et-domaines-dinteret/principaux-changements-loi-25):

- **Privacy officer:** by default the business's highest authority, delegable in writing; title and contact details must be published on the website.
- **Incidents:** confidentiality incidents posing a risk of serious harm must be reported to the Commission and to affected people; an incident register must be kept.
- **Consent (since September 2023):** must be manifest, free, informed and specific, requested separately for each purpose in plain language.
- **Transfers outside Quebec:** a privacy impact assessment and a written agreement are required before transferring personal information outside Quebec.
- **Portability (since 2024-09-22):** personal information must be provided in a structured, commonly used format on request.
- **Penalties:** administrative penalties up to $10 million or 2% of worldwide revenue.

**Unverified:** The Commission's page does not say whether Law 25 applies to a business based outside Quebec. **Recommendation:** An Ontario business that actively markets to or collects data from Quebec residents should get legal advice; publishing a privacy officer contact and per-purpose consent are low-cost steps either way.

## Storing customer data outside Canada (data residency)

From the OPC's [Guidelines for processing personal data across borders](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/airports-and-borders/gl_dab_090127/) (modified 2009-01-27):

- PIPEDA **does not prohibit** sending personal information to a service provider in another country (for example a US-hosted CRM or email platform). It is treated as a "use", so no extra consent is needed if it is for the original purpose.
- The business **remains accountable** and should protect the data by contract, with a comparable level of protection.
- Customers must be told their information may be processed in another country and may be accessible to that country's courts, law enforcement and national security authorities, ideally at collection.

**Note:** The OPC guideline is from 2009 and is still the page the OPC publishes on this topic. Quebec's Law 25 adds an assessment requirement for transfers outside Quebec (above), and CRA record location rules are in [federal-requirements.md](federal-requirements.md).

**Recommendation:** Add one sentence to your privacy policy naming the countries where your main tools store data, and prefer vendors offering Canadian hosting for health or financial data.
